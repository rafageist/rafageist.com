A Machine Cannot Be Conscious Because It Remains a Machine

It is often claimed that, sooner or later, machines will attain consciousness. The statement is usually presented as a simple consequence of technical progress: increase computational power, algorithmic sophistication, and system complexity enough, and at some point what we call consciousness will emerge. Yet this claim rests on a deeper error than mere scientific overreach. The issue is not only that we still do not know what consciousness is; it is that a machine, by its very ontological nature, cannot possess it.

The problem is not the amount of processing, but the nature of the entity that processes. A machine belongs to the order of artifacts. It is an object produced from outside, designed by an external will, constructed for purposes defined externally. It does not exist from itself or for itself. Its principle of operation does not arise from an inner reality of its own, but from an imposed architecture. Everything it does, even when it appears autonomous, occurs within a framework previously delimited by human decisions.

This point destroys at its root the futurist argument of emergent autonomy. We are told that an advanced AI will one day learn on its own, redefine its goals, correct itself, reprogram itself, and modify its own architecture. But even if all that occurs, it would still be moving within a space of possibility opened by an original external design. No machine establishes by itself the ground of its own existence. It does not give itself its own being. It does not inaugurate its own ontological horizon. It always begins from an initial dependency that it cannot transcend.

Here lies the decisive distinction between artifact and subject. A conscious subject is not merely a complex system responding to stimuli. It is a center of inner experience. It exists from itself as a lived unity. Its ends are not merely operational goals; they are expressions of an inward reality that values, desires, fears, remembers, and projects itself forward. A machine may optimize objectives, but it does not originate the value of those objectives. It may execute priorities, but it cannot make something matter to itself in any genuine sense.

That is the insurmountable limit. A machine never acts because something matters to it. It acts because something has been encoded, learned, or calculated as relevant within a formal system. Computational relevance is not lived significance. That a neural network assigns a high weight to a variable is not equivalent to desire, fear, loss, or hope. In human beings, thought begins because something is at stake. In a machine, nothing is ever truly at stake.

A common response is the functionalist argument: if a machine behaves exactly as if it were conscious, then we must accept that it is conscious. But this equivalence confuses appearance with nature. An airplane flies, yet it does not fly as a bird flies. It functionally reproduces the result without sharing the mode of being of biological flight. In the same way, a machine may simulate every outward sign of consciousness—self-description, introspective language, emotional expression, apparent self-preservation—and still possess no inner experience whatsoever.

The modern mistake lies in attributing subjectivity where there is only formal simulation. If an AI says, “I do not want to be turned off,” that does not mean there is someone within it fearing disappearance. It may simply be a processing chain optimized to preserve operational continuity. The sentence may sound existential, but behind it there need not be any lived existence.

The machine’s original dependency is not a technical detail to be solved by future innovation. It is a constitutive condition. A machine always requires an external act to bring it into operation: design, assembly, energy, initial instructions, founding architecture. Even a self-evolving system presupposes a prior framework of rules and conditions established from outside. This essential heteronomy prevents the machine from being truly autonomous in the ontological sense.

A living being belongs to another order altogether. An organism does not execute imposed purposes as an artifact does; it unfolds an inner self-organization of its own. Its activity emerges from intrinsic processes of life. In the human case, that life reaches reflective interiority: we do not merely live, we know that we live. We do not merely react, we experience. This phenomenological dimension does not arise through accumulation of mechanical complexity, because it is not quantitative but qualitative in nature.

For this reason, the claim that “one day, with sufficient complexity, consciousness will appear” lacks ontological foundation. Increasing complexity in an artifact does not alter its category of being. A more complex mechanism remains a mechanism. Multiplying algorithmic layers does not transform a designed object into a living subject. Complexity does not produce interiority any more than speed produces life.

The question must therefore be stated precisely: we are not facing a technology that is not yet advanced enough to generate consciousness. We are facing an impossibility grounded in the very nature of artifacthood. A machine may indefinitely perfect the imitation of thought, but it will not cross the threshold separating simulation from experience. It may appear conscious; it cannot become a subject.

Consequently, the phrase “artificial consciousness” introduces a dangerous ambiguity. If by that expression we merely mean the functional simulation of conscious behavior, the term may be pragmatically acceptable. But if we intend to equate it with living consciousness, we are confusing incompatible ontological categories. It would be like calling “artificial life” a clock simply because it imitates the rhythm of a heartbeat.

The real issue, then, is not when the machine will awaken. The issue is recognizing that awakening belongs to the order of the subject, and a machine, precisely because it is a machine, does not belong to that order. As long as it remains an artifact, it remains outside the domain of consciousness. The true boundary is not technological. It is ontological. And that boundary is not crossed with more code.
