Back to 1960 ‐ AI is forcing us to take specifications seriously again

Spec driven development is being presented today as a new idea, but it is closer to a return than an invention. If you go back to the origins of software engineering in the late 1960s, especially around the NATO Software Engineering Conference, the core problem was already clear. Systems were failing not because people could not write code, but because they did not define clearly what the system should do. The answer at that time was simple in principle, even if hard in practice: define the behavior first, then implement it.

Researchers like C. A. R. Hoare and Edsger W. Dijkstra made this idea explicit. They showed that a program is not just code. It is a set of guarantees. Preconditions, postconditions, invariants. In other words, a specification. From there, the industry built methods and standards that tried to bring that discipline into real projects. Processes like Rational Unified Process insisted on clear requirements, models, and traceability. Standards such as IEEE 830 and later ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 formalized how requirements should be written and maintained. None of this was optional in serious environments. It was the baseline for building systems that had to work.

Over time, practice drifted away from that rigor. Agile methods improved many things, but in everyday use they were often reduced to short cycles and informal definitions. Specifications became lighter, sometimes just tickets or conversations. For human teams this was manageable. People could fill the gaps, interpret intent, and correct mistakes along the way.

AI changes that dynamic. A machine does not fill gaps the way a human does. It expands whatever you give it. If the input is vague, the output will be consistently vague. If the requirements are incomplete, the system you generate will reflect that incompleteness at scale. This is where the lack of specification stops being a minor issue and becomes a structural problem.

This is also why more rigorous approaches are gaining attention again. Not just classic requirements documents, but formal reasoning as well. Tools and methods like TLA+ bring the idea to its logical extreme. You define the system in terms of states and transitions, and you check its correctness before writing the implementation. This was always possible, but often seen as too heavy. Now, with AI generating large amounts of code quickly, the cost of not having that level of clarity is becoming more visible.

What we are seeing is not a new paradigm. It is a correction. The industry is being pushed back to a simple truth that was already known decades ago: you cannot separate good software from clear specifications. The difference is that today the consequences of ignoring that truth scale much faster. AI does not forgive ambiguity. It amplifies it.

Spec driven development is not the future. It is a return to the foundations of software engineering. The only real novelty is that now we have tools that make both good and bad specifications propagate much further than before.
