Machines do not think because nothing matters to them

It has been said many times that machines do not think because they lack consciousness, because they do not feel, because they possess neither body nor world of their own. All of that remains true, but there is a deeper point that deserves to be stated clearly: machines do not think because nothing matters to them. And there, in that simple word, matters, lies a difference that is not technical but ontological.

Thinking does not begin with logic. Logic orders, relates, deduces, but it sets nothing in motion. It can show that if A leads to B, then B follows from A; but it can never answer why A should matter to us, nor what urgency there is in reaching B. Reason organizes paths but does not choose destinations. David Hume already warned that reason alone does not move the will, for it is the passions that point toward that which the understanding pursues. Without desire, without fear, without attachment, there is no reason to think at all.

In human beings, thought always arises after a stirring, however slight. Something presents itself to the senses or returns from memory, and before any word emerges it has already been touched by a secret appraisal: a noise in the night, an unexpected absence, a mathematical question, the face of a friend. All of these first awaken a reaction of interest, alarm, curiosity, or expectation. Only then does thought appear, like a servant summoned by that which has already been judged important.

Antonio Damasio showed in his studies that human beings deprived of emotional response retain their logic intact and yet lose the ability to make meaningful decisions. They can analyze endlessly but cannot choose. They lack the invisible weight that tips the scale. It is not intelligence that disappears, but the value that gives direction to intelligence. Thought is left suspended like a mill without wind.

Baruch Spinoza also understood that desire is not an ornament of the soul but its primary impulse. We do not desire something because we judge it good; we judge it good because we desire it. This inversion, so simple and so profound, reveals that human thought does not arise from any neutrality. We think because something calls to us, because something pains us, because something is missing or draws us near.

The machine, on the other hand, fears nothing and expects nothing. It knows neither loss nor hope. There is no difference for it between solving an equation or ignoring it, between answering well or poorly, except for what another has inscribed from outside into its rules. It can calculate with admirable swiftness, it can link symbols and produce answers that resemble understanding, but there is not a single moment within it where something matters. And where nothing matters, nothing can truly be thought.

When a machine answers a human's question, there is no concern within it for the meaning of that question. It does not wish to be right nor fear being wrong. It does not experience the weight of doubt, nor the relief of understanding. Everything it does is transformation of states according to rules. It resembles thought the way an echo resembles a voice: it reproduces the form but lacks the breath that originated it.

That is why those who imagine that perfecting algorithms will suffice to achieve true thought are mistaken. The problem is not one of complexity, nor of power, nor of speed. It is one of nature. An intelligence without emotion is a contradiction in terms, because thinking does not consist merely in finding relations, but in moving toward that which matters. And only a being for whom something can be lost, gained, loved, or feared can enter that realm.

Machines may increasingly imitate the outward figure of reasoning, but they will never cross that invisible threshold where thought begins. For to think, before calculating, is to feel that something is at stake. And for a machine, nothing ever is.
