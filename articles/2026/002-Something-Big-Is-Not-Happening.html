Something Big Is Not Happening

Every day we hear that something huge is happening: that artificial intelligence is growing out of control, that it will soon surpass human beings, and that we are facing a point of no return. But maybe we are looking at the wrong variable. The dominant narrative usually relies on a simple comparison: X(t) as the functional capability of AI and H(t) as the capacity for human thought, so collective fear is summed up as ∃ t : X(t) > H(t). That moment is described as the day the machine surpasses us, even though the model is incomplete because it assumes, without proving it, that H(t) remains stable and only X(t) changes.

This is not a new suspicion: it keeps resurfacing in my previous essays questioning the idea that machines think, the false notion of recursive self improvement, and the confusion between producing outputs and understanding.

Here is the part that is almost never discussed: we cannot measure exactly how much H(t) is worth, but we can observe cultural signals of its evolution. If dX/dt > 0 while dH/dt < 0, the distance between both is reduced for two different reasons: the machine improves and the human delegates. In that scenario, the point where X = H does not require artificial consciousness; it can be reached simply through a reduction in human cognitive exercise. That is why the same crossing can be told in two opposite ways: as extraordinary AI progress or as a gradual decline in the human cognitive standard.

This difference in interpretation matters because thinking is not automatic. For a long time, a simple useful idea has been repeated: the brain is like a muscle; if it is not trained, it weakens. Each generation has more powerful tools, but that does not mean we are training our minds any better. Thinking requires sustained attention, active memory, tolerance for uncertainty, and the ability to ask questions without immediate answers. Today's technology is designed to remove friction: it offers instant responses and reduces the need to remember, calculate, or structure arguments from scratch. That is not bad in itself; the problem appears when assistance replaces exercise instead of complementing it.

From there, it is also useful to distinguish output from process. An artificial system implements something like f(x) → y: input, numerical transformation, output. Human thinking includes layers that are harder to formalize, such as doubt, intention, evaluation, responsibility, and lived context. So even if f(x) = g(x), it does not follow that f and g are the same process. Two systems can produce the same answer and still be radically different in their nature.

Seen this way, the main risk shifts: it is not that the machine wakes up, but that the human mind falls asleep. If education trains repetition instead of understanding, if culture rewards speed over depth, and if we systematically delegate cognitive effort, then H(t) declines without us noticing. When someone finally says AI has surpassed human beings, the key question will be: which human are we comparing it to? If the standard has been declining for decades, that crossing will not be a technological miracle, but a cultural consequence.

The real urgency, then, is not to decide whether the machine thinks, but to verify whether we still do. Human intelligence cannot be outsourced and cannot be automated: it can only be cultivated. Maybe something big is happening, yes, but it is not necessarily the birth of an artificial mind; it may be the silent erosion of the human mind. If delegating thinking becomes a habit, will it still make sense to ask whether the machine thinks?
