When engineering degrees are awarded without teaching engineering

From my experience working with developers of different generations and countries, participating in technical interviews, reviewing academic and professional profiles, and accompanying learning processes as a mentor, I have observed a quiet but deep problem: educational programs that grant Computer Engineering degrees without training engineers. This is not a debate about talent, individual effort, or vocation, but a structural failure in how we understand and teach what engineering really is.

The most visible symptom appears in technical interviews and early professional practice. People who present themselves as junior engineers cannot follow the logic of a simple program, cannot explain what a bit is or how information flows in a system, and show a total disconnect from the basic principles of computing. This is not a lack of work experience; it is an absence of conceptual training.

Another clear indicator is the absence of engineering thinking. They do not know development methodologies, cannot model a system before implementing it, have not seen UML or other abstraction tools, and cannot describe a solution in a structured way. Faced with a real problem, they do not reason in terms of components, responsibilities, constraints, and consequences, but try to "do something" without understanding the full system.

Added to this is the lack of a minimal mathematical foundation. This is not about demanding pure mathematicians, but about recognizing that all engineering rests on logic, abstraction, relations, functions, and formal reasoning. Without that mental language, there is no possible engineering, only a superficial use of technology.

In recent years another aggravating factor has appeared: the use of artificial intelligence tools as a substitute for thinking. Many people claim to "know AI" when in fact they only know how to use it. They do not understand how these systems are built, do not know propositional logic, do not handle concept maps or mental models, and are unaware of even the basic concepts behind models like neural networks. They confuse the generation of results with the understanding of the problem. The tool becomes a cognitive crutch that hides the lack of fundamentals.

This lack of training becomes especially evident when asked to describe a real solution. Faced with a basic question about a web application, some cannot model a simple database, do not understand many-to-many relationships (let alone normalize a database), cannot explain what the backend or frontend would do, and much less consider essential aspects like monitoring, analytics, security, standards, or maintenance. It is not that they have not worked yet; it is that they were never taught to think in complete systems.

The common pattern behind all these symptoms is clear: they were taught to use tools, but not to think like an engineer. Visible execution was prioritized over invisible reasoning. They were taught to produce artifacts, not to justify decisions. That is not engineering.

Using technology is not the same as understanding it. Using a washing machine does not make anyone a washing machine mechanic. In the same way, using frameworks, languages, or modern tools does not automatically make a person an engineer. The engineer understands principles, diagnoses problems, designs solutions, and takes responsibility for decisions.

This problem does not only affect individuals; it affects the entire market. It generates frustration in interviews, premature salary conflicts, narratives of "the market is broken" or "AI will replace us," and a growing disconnect between what companies need and what certain institutions claim to produce. Artificial intelligence is not the cause of this phenomenon; it simply exposes it faster.

Training engineers means teaching them to think before doing, to model before implementing, and to understand before executing. Without that, any degree is only a label, and what is presented as engineering is reduced to something easily automatable.
