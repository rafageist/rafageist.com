Why Companies Fail When They Stop Training Engineers

Software companies that do well in the long run share a quiet but fundamental trait: the coexistence of different generations of engineers. It is not about age, but about experience, role, and stage of professional growth. When that balance exists, knowledge flows, judgment is passed on, and the organization builds a future. When it breaks, the company can keep producing for a while, but it does so on an increasingly fragile base.

Software engineering is not a discipline that can be mastered instantly. It is not enough to know a language, a framework, or a trendy tool. Training as an engineer means developing a way of thinking: learning to model problems, understand complex systems, reason about consequences, and assume responsibility for technical decisions. That process begins, ideally, at the university, where theoretical foundations, intellectual discipline, and a first formal approach to computational thinking are acquired.

A good university education does not produce engineers who are "ready," but it does produce engineers who are trainable. It provides a common language, basic concepts, and the mental framework needed to grow professionally. When companies dismiss academic training or consider it irrelevant, they are not being pragmatic; they are weakening their own foundation. Without fundamentals, all later learning becomes fragile and dependent on fads or fleeting tools.

That is why the link between university and industry is not a luxury or an institutional formality, but a structural necessity. Universities train in theory and method; companies train in context, practice, and responsibility. When both sides collaborate, the transition from student to junior engineer stops being abrupt and becomes a natural process. When that link does not exist, companies complain about the lack of talent while ignoring their own role in its formation.

Junior engineers are not cheap or replaceable resources. They are an investment in the future. Their function is not only to produce code, but to learn to think like engineers within real systems, with real constraints and real consequences. For that to happen, they need something no tool can offer: human guidance. They need role models who explain not only what to do, but why it is done that way and not another.

Here the role of more experienced engineers comes into play. Senior profiles add value not only through their individual productivity, but through their ability to transmit judgment, detect errors before they happen, and put technical decisions in context. They are the living memory of the organization. A company that does without them, or keeps them isolated from the younger ones, loses something more valuable than speed: it loses accumulated learning.

Mentorship is the mechanism that connects these generations. It can be informal or structured, internal or external, funded by the company or sought privately. Its form can vary, but its function is always the same: to accelerate the formation of judgment. A mentor does not replace experience, but helps avoid unnecessary errors, understand problems better, and develop a broader view of the craft.

When companies do not have internal capacity to mentor - whether due to time pressure, lack of suitable profiles, or weak technical cultures - external mentorship becomes a legitimate and valuable alternative. It is not a sign of failure, but of maturity: recognizing that training engineers requires time, attention, and knowledge that are not always available internally.

Organizations that refuse to hire juniors or that eliminate training processes are not being efficient; they are externalizing a cost that they will inevitably pay later. Without a growth pipeline, they will depend on constant hiring, high turnover, and continuous loss of knowledge. The apparent productivity of the present becomes structural fragility in the future.

The coexistence of different generations of engineers is not a sentimental or cultural issue. It is invisible infrastructure. It is what allows knowledge not to be lost, decisions to be understood, and systems to evolve without collapsing. No tool, however advanced, can replace that human process.

Investing in training, strengthening the link with the university, and promoting mentorship are not optional expenses. They are the minimum conditions for a software company to not only function today, but remain viable tomorrow. Where engineering thinking is preserved and transmitted, software improves. Where that chain is broken, no technological shortcut can make up for it.
