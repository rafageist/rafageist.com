Why Replacing Software Engineers with AI Is a Category Error

In 2025 it has become common to hear that software engineers - first juniors, but increasingly at any level - can be replaced by "artificial intelligence." This idea is often presented as a natural continuation of historical automation, comparable to industrialization or mechanization of work. That comparison is deeply mistaken. What is happening today is not a new stage of automation, but a conceptual error about what machines are and what it really means to do engineering.

I have already argued in another text that machines do not think and cannot think. This article does not repeat that reasoning. It takes it as given. The goal here is not to debate whether machines will ever think, but to explain why trying to substitute software engineers with tools called "AI" is conceptually incorrect from the start, regardless of how advanced those tools are.

The first problem is in the name. What is today called "artificial intelligence" is not intelligence. It is automated analysis of information: pattern extraction, statistical correlation, and probabilistic generation of results. These systems do not understand problems, have no intention, do not handle meaning, and do not distinguish between success and error in a human sense. They transform inputs into outputs following learned distributions. A more honest name would be "probabilistic analysis of information" or "statistical generation of patterns." The term "intelligence" does not describe what they do; it disguises it.

This distinction is crucial because software engineering is not about producing code. Code is an artifact. Engineering is the intellectual process that leads to it. An engineer - junior, senior, or architect - does not add value by typing faster, but by framing problems, understanding context, evaluating constraints, making conscious trade-offs, and assuming responsibility for consequences. None of that is a mechanical operation that can be automated, because they are not formal transformations, but acts of judgment.

Historical automation replaced humans where they were weak: physical strength, endurance, repetition, speed. Machines lift more weight, move faster, calculate better, and do not get tired. That was progress precisely because those limitations never defined the human being. Thinking, by contrast, is not a technical limitation to overcome. It is a constitutive condition of human work in engineering. Trying to replace it is not an evolution of automation; it is a category mistake.

When it is said that a junior engineer can be replaced by AI, what is really being said is that their role is believed to consist solely of producing artifacts. That is false. The junior's role is to learn to think like an engineer. When the same is said of a senior, the error is even more evident: accumulated experience is confused with accumulated production. In both cases, engineering is reduced to visible deliverables and the invisible intellectual process that makes them possible is ignored.

Current tools can generate code that compiles, passes tests, and even works in limited scenarios. But producing a correct result does not imply understanding why it is correct. Two systems can generate the same output by following completely different processes. That a machine produces a functional solution does not mean it understands the problem it supposedly solves. That difference is not quantitative; it is categorical.

This distinction becomes critical in production systems. Production is not a technical environment; it is a domain of responsibility. It involves real users, real data, legal obligations, economic impacts, and reputational risks. Approving software for production is not a syntactic verification. It is a professional and ethical act. A machine cannot assume responsibility, cannot be held accountable, and cannot even recognize failure as failure. That is why it cannot replace an engineer at any level.

The rise of this narrative is not neutral. It is driven by economic interests: justification for cuts, inflated valuations, and maintenance of investment bubbles. Like any bubble, it depends on exaggerating capabilities and minimizing limits. The more one tries to remove human judgment from engineering, the more fragile systems become and the greater the human intervention needed to fix them afterward.

Replacing software engineers with "AI" is not only impractical; it is conceptually incoherent. It starts from the assumption that thinking is a function, that judgment is a result, and that responsibility is optional. None of those assumptions is valid. The problem is not that machines are not yet good enough. The problem is that the question itself is wrongly framed.

Artificial intelligence does not replace engineers. It replaces the illusion that engineering is a mechanical activity. When that illusion dissipates, what remains is what has always been there: people thinking, deciding, doubting, and taking responsibility. That is not a relic of the past. It is the very foundation of software engineering, and it cannot be automated.
